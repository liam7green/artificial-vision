# A Framework for Artificial Human Vision
Liam Green, Blair Academy Integrated Science Research (2025)

## Abstract
Recent advances in deep neural networks (DNNs) have produced promising results in modeling cortical responses of the brain. However, these neural networks remain as elusive as the brain, just another “black box” layer to peer into because successful predictions of brain activity don’t automatically explain *why* or *how*. To overcome this challenge, a biologically plausible model is designed with constraints inspired by known cortical organization and behaviors, trained under ecological-based conditions using naturalistic stimuli. In this approach, closer alignment with the actual brain can produce more interpretable, layer-by-layer computations. The framework processes visual features based on the behaviors of the retina and lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) in the brain. The visual encoder models temporal dynamics in cortical processing, given historical context from visual information before generating the final whole-brain response, an artificial representation of the human sense of sight. The results of the encoding model have achieved a high relative accuracy of 90% in predicting human sight. The design of the framework presents how different regions of interest (ROI) in the brain correspond to the particular activations that encode for visual perception. Together, this model highlights the potential of the encoding software that will power prosthetics to restore sight to the blind.  

## Introduction
The human mind has long been the last frontier of exploration, and understanding the behavior of the brain in constructing visual perception, the sense of sight, is one of neuroscience’s greatest ambitions. Current research explores the function of the brain computationally, often using deep neural networks (DNN) to predict responses from certain areas in the brain or to replicate specific dynamics. DNNs have been trained for frame-by-frame prediction from visual stimuli, largely producing significant results with an emphasis on spatial dynamics.<sup>1</sup> However, the lack of temporal awareness and exposure to natural scenes (often traded off for synthetic image stimuli) makes it difficult to create a plausible representation of the human visual system, yielding predictions that aren’t dynamic to the real world.  

This research instead proposes a neuromorphic engineering approach to modeling human vision: using biology as a guide for computing. Naturalistic stimuli, such as movies, offer diverse and dynamic imitations of the natural world, mimicking conditions of perceptual and cognitive experiences experienced in everyday life, even in a controlled study. Recording fMRI response to such visual stimuli can offer a powerful representation of the human sense of sight, particularly when studying regions-of-interest (ROIs) associated with vision.<sup>2</sup>
Neural networks are not analogous to the brain, despite the deceptively similar nomenclature used to describe deep learning technology. While the structures of DNNs are loosely based on biological neurons, there is hardly any evidence that the brain computes in the same way as neural networks.3 Therefore, the proposed encoding model aims to bridge the gap between artificial and biological neural networks by replicating the structure and behavior of the brain and its visual pathway.  

To model neural encoding and artificial vision, the biological logic of cortical processing must be defined and differentiated into a regional hierarchy, from the retina to the occipital lobe. After the brain receives information from its surroundings, raw signals travel down the optic nerve and tract to the LGN, the first highlighted region of visual processing. Here, the three cell types extract features for movement, shape, and color information, respectively.<sup>4</sup> Next, these features move into the occipital lobe, where the visual cortex codes for perceived sight. It is crucial to acknowledge the temporal nature of the encoding process, where both visual stimuli and neural response are dynamic: not only do one’s surroundings constantly change, but the brain’s activations to even a static image change over time.<sup>5</sup> Therefore, any given snapshot of neural activity must be analyzed in the context of preceding stimuli to capture the temporal dynamics of the brain.  

In this approach to visual perception through the lens of the brain’s natural architecture and behavior, the full potential of artificial neural networks can be oriented towards accurately predicting and recreating the human mind: how patterns and learnable behaviors of different neurons in the cortex come together to create consciousness, and in this case, the sense of sight. The results of the encoding model highlight the potential for translating digitally recorded video of the external world into neural activations interpretable by the human brain in the pursuit of curing blindness through artificial visual perception.  

## Methods
The encoding model is designed to be trained on high spatial and temporal resolution task-based fMRI data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP), measuring the brain’s response to viewing naturalistic stimuli.<sup>6</sup> The model studies the cortical responses of 11 subjects to 4 diverse movies. All fMRI is preprocessed according to the HCP pipeline and registered to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template space.<sup>7</sup> A study of the HCP movie-watching dataset estimates a hemodynamic delay of 4 seconds to return the most accurate encoding performance.<sup>8</sup> Because of this lag between stimulus onset and the change in blood flow measured by the BOLD signal, the encoding model predicts the brain activation 4 seconds after the presented visual input. Furthermore, due to the differences in measured intensity across fMRI timeseries, the preprocessed fMRI (Y) is also feature-scaled using min-max normalization for each voxel:  

$$Y_{scaled} = \frac{Y - Y_{min}}{Y_{max} - Y_{min}}$$

The data loader collects the last frame of every second of the videos as the visual stimulus, inputted as a sequence of 20 images for a total of 20 seconds of temporal context. The model is trained on 3 of the 4 movies and optimized to minimize the mean squared error (MSE) of the predicted and the target fMRI values. Figure 1 below describes the framework of the predictive model.

**Figure 1:** Model framework. The encoder inputs a series of naturalistic images that feed into a feature extraction block based on convolutional neural networks (CNN). The processed hierarchical feature sequence moves into a recurrent block using a long short-term memory network (LSTM). The combined output is fed into the brain response model that predicts a whole-brain fMRI volume in response to the stimuli.

There are three main components of the encoding model, each designed to replicate a specific structure or behavior of the brain. To express the heavy feature processing from the retinas, down the visual pathway, through the LGN, and into the visual cortex, a rich feature pyramid network (FPN) is employed after learning low-level components from the powerful ResNet-50 CNN, pretrained with strong performance as a foundational architecture.<sup>9</sup> The next block uses an LSTM to process 20 seconds of features leading up to the predicted fMRI to model temporal dynamics in the brain. The time-rich data then moves into the brain response model, where 3D convolutions aim to replicate the occipital lobe and the visual cortex’s role in generating sight in the mind.

After training for 50 epochs and achieving convergence where the loss stabilized at a local minimum, the model was evaluated on the fourth movie, unseen in the training data. For quantitative evaluation, accuracy was calculated using the mean absolute percent error between the actual (At) and predicted fMRI activations (Pt):

$$MAPE = 100\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left|\frac{A_t - P_t}{A_t}\right|$$

In testing, the actual fMRI voxel values in At were derived from the group-averaged neural activations across all 11 subjects watching movie 4. 

## Results
In normalizing the fMRI for training, 74% of voxels returned a divide-by-zero error, where Y<sub>max</sub> – Y<sub>min</sub> = 0. These voxels were largely present in regions that do not process vision, remaining the same throughout the entire timeseries (Figure 2). Therefore, only results in the visual regions, which have learnable dynamics in their changing activations, are considered in the analysis of the model’s accuracy.

**Figure 2:** Annotated heatmap of active regions in a subject’s whole brain fMRI volume. Voxels with higher saturation show the most change across the entire timeseries, and regions of significance are highlighted. As labeled, the visual cortices and lateral geniculate nuclei on both hemispheres show significant change.

Simply calculating the whole-brain encoding performance on new visual stimuli (MAPE = 9.76%) would defeat the purpose of interpreting the influence of certain modalities, such as hierarchical feature extraction and temporal dynamics, on predicting visual perception. Therefore, anatomical ROIs are derived from the fMRI volumes to compute predictive accuracy at known brain areas responsible for visual processing. All ROIs are parcellated by HCP’s 1.6mm volumetric atlas and comprehensively differentiated into four groups of study: the primary visual cortex (V1), the ventral stream, the dorsal stream, and the thalamus, which includes the LGN (Figure 3A).<sup>2</sup>

ROIs associated with the thalamus, containing the lateral geniculate nucleus, show the highest encoding accuracy of all visual regions (Figure 3B). This level of performance agrees with the idea of hierarchical feature processing in vision. Furthermore, the ventral and dorsal streams are widely considered the two visual pathways or systems after leaving the visual cortex: the ventral stream generally computes for identification and recognition, and the dorsal stream coordinates visual information for action and visually-guided motor behaviors.<sup>10</sup> Under the experimental model’s framework, though, the predicted dorsal stream results report a higher overall accuracy. While slight, the difference is still a significant metric to discuss. Last, the primary visual cortex, the center of visual processing, showed relatively lower performance compared to the other ROIs, but it still demonstrated meaningful predictive accuracy.

## Discussion
In studying the brain’s stimulus-driven response to naturalistic conditions plausible to everyday life, a proposed deep neural network succeeds in modeling the behavior of structures of the visual system. Driving the learning of cortical function was the design of computational structures corresponding with regions and dynamics of the brain, including a full hierarchical feature pyramid and a temporal block. As a result, the model was largely able to learn how humans see the world, replicating visual perception in the form of fMRI volumes measuring neuronal activity in regions across the brain. 

The accuracy of different regions of interest, while all high and relatively similar, gives quantitative insight into how well the model replicates sensory-driven behavior. For example, the large emphasis on feature extraction and processing caused the LGN to yield the highest overall accuracy (Figure 3B). With differentiated anatomical features that are naturally made for preprocessing visual information, the evaluation results agree with the conclusion that this model has rigorously imitated the processing aspect of vision.<sup>11</sup> The emphasis on CNNs especially produces rich feature maps because computer vision, unrelated to neuroscience, is one of the most widely researched fields in machine learning.

Two different pathways of visual perception, the ventral and the dorsal stream, diverge in accuracy, which can be explained when comparing the two systems (Figure 3B). In studying both streams, researchers have found that the dorsal stream is more sensitive to temporal dynamics, due to its nature in processing vision in coordination with motion.10 Meanwhile, the ventral stream focuses on generating a spatial map of object and scene perception. The encoding model’s addition of an LSTM block boosts the accuracy of the dorsal stream’s region with its rich temporal information to guide its visuomotor function over time.

Together, the ventral and dorsal stream stem from the visual cortex, where V1 performed slightly below the average whole-brain MAPE, however, still achieving an error of only 10%. In all movie-watching fMRI volumes, voxels near the visual cortex and LGN were the most dynamic and responsive to stimuli. Where state-of-the-art CNN feature extraction may have helped the model learn the LGN’s behavior, the conversion to fMRI and visual perception in the brain response model likely needs a higher volume of diverse data to generalize from, since V1 is central to generating basic human sight.

Besides the overall success of the encoding model, the voxel-wise min-max normalization of all fMRI training data decreased the richness of neuronal information, where some voxels could not be used for analysis (Figure 2). It’s possible that the zero-value voxels made it more difficult for the encoder to learn the overall behavior of the brain, even if given working values in stimulus-driven regions. Future approaches to this research can investigate methods of normalizing and preprocessing neuroimaging data that don’t harm its quality. Another source 
Furthermore, it’s important to recognize that even with a DNN specifically designed for the visual system, neural networks are inherently different from their biological counterparts.3 While the artificial neuron was invented loosely based on the biological neuron decades ago, researchers have consistently disproved their relationship. While sufficient training data and a large encoding model may be enough to perfect the prediction of artificial human sight, a dedicated refresh to the decades-old artificial neuron, or even a new paradigm that better models biological networks, is the next direction of this field of research.

Overall, this study demonstrates that predicting the brain’s exact behavior under biologically plausible conditions can be achieved with relatively low error by modeling DNNs with modalities based on widely studied features of cortical function. Particularly in the role of visual perception, this model marks a foundational point for the future of directly interfacing with the brain. Once DNNs can reliably understand and decrypt the natural coding of the human mind, only engineering neuroprosthetic brain-computer interfaces stand in the way of curing blindness with an end-to-end translation system from digital video to neural code.
